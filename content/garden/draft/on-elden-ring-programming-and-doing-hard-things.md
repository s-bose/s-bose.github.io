## Introduction

I have started playing Elden Ring recently. I wasn't a big fan of souls-like games initially, and constantly dying to Margit on my first run brought me this close to bashing my keyboard.
But lately I decided to give it another go, and instead of treating the game like a chore I thought of slowing down, enjoying the bits and pieces, and taking in the environment, and boy did that help. Modern gaming, as with most other entertainment formats, have become a constant feedback loop of dopamine rush. Rushing to cross out one checkpoint after the next, leveling up fast or speedrunning to the next best weapon, or scrolling through the reels and fast-forwarding endlessly to extract as much as we can, our attention span has been reduced down to an insulting amount. And that's why the thing that made me fall in love with Elden Ring more than the art direction, game mechanics, or the story, is that it taught me two things: patience, and acceptance. And just like art imitates life, I believe some of these have very interesting parallels to programming, or software development in general.


## Gitting Gud

> Git gud, a slang rendering of get good, refers to getting better at a task or skill, used especially among video gamers online.
> (Source: dictionary.com)

It is a quite well-known fact that souls-like games don't hold your hands. Neither does it exposition dump the lore on your face. You are supposed to figure out the world, the mechanics, the bosses, on your own. If you can't, well that sucks, git gud. Very few games do that. I haven't played a lot of games but most games (I'm emphasizing on single-player games) focus on storytelling and the core experience they offer is the narrative, the combat, mechanics and level-design are mere tools to assist with the narrative. In Elden Ring, the struggle **is** the point. The narrative and the world-building instead offers to assist with that, and give context to that. You are just a foul Tarnished, a maidenless runt, who is on a foolish quest to something great, to acquire the Elden Ring, and you **are** going to get your ass handed to you, because IT IS challenging. But not impossible. There will be naysayers, people who don't believe in you, ridicule you. But you have to soldier on and embrace the suck.

I feel like the journey of programming is kinda similar. I am not talking about the certain minorities who are prodigies but the majority of us who learned to program and genuinely struggled with it. The cool part about it is that there's never a shortage of challenges, the so-called "suffering". In a very masochistic way, the joy of programming is not in the number of projects built, or the number of languages/frameworks mastered, but in the process of debugging, navigating through error logs and traces, reading the docs, searching Stackoverflow (RIP), followed by the short burst of happiness when one thing that was bugging you for a week was finally solved, but then you move on to the next, and the next, and the next. The "suck" was what kept you going, and there is something so human about this.
We humans love to do hard things. Because why not! 

As John F. Kennedy said:

> We choose to go to the Moon in this decade and do the other things, not because they are easy, but because they are hard.

If we hadn't decided to try the forbidden fruit, take a leap of faith, challenge the conventions, our society wouldn't be at a stage where it is. In software engineering too, we have consistently raised the bars, building abstractions on top of abstractions to take on bigger and juicier challenges of software. There is no KPI to measure, no incentive to motivate, but the love of the game.

The shift to agentic coding today, I feel like has diminished the human element of it. I am not a luddite, I use Claude daily for a lot of stuff and it truly is a marvel of modern engineering considering how much time it saves and how fast the software development process has become. But it feels more like slapping a microtransaction tier that lets you complete a game in half an hour. It is also true that your job does not have to fulfill your hobbies, and the tech industry is quickly heading in a dirrection where writing code by hand is the bottleneck, and rapid prototyping is the future. But allow me to draw a parallel to video games again, writing code, thinking in the programming language was the core mechanic of the game. When you strip that away, you are left with simply progressing through the storyline with your hands off the controller, kinda like the new Wolverine game.

What does this mean for software? Nothing much really. Production software is boring, reliable. As long as the AI-generated code is verified, tested with humans in the decision-making loop, and the core components are functional, we are still gonna continue building good software. The key here is humans in the decision loop, humans who have the scar-tissue of years of breaking prod, building sub-optimal solutions and reiterating over them.

But for someone who is just starting out in the industry, this is detrimental to their personal growth, especially if they are passionate about programming. We currently have very few opportunities for a junior to "git gud" through the trials and tribulations of writing software, and the it is very easy and cheap to be tempted by the "coding is solved" tools. And it might also feel very lonely and pointless to build things on your own when Fable can one-shot your dream website in `localhost:3000`.

But there is goodness in the suck, in writing abysmal code that barely compiles and traceback errors that keep you up at night. There is value in reading the documentations, blogs of other humans and decades-old stackoverflow posts with negative votes. Because the struggle **is** the point, much like the beatdowns in souls games.

More importantly, if not for the love of the challenges of building software, this process would build the agency and judgement for good and bad code that would improve the feedback loop of agentic engineering at work. 


## Patience

![](https://cdn.kpbs.org/dims4/default/064991e/2147483647/strip/true/crop/1495x921+71+0/resize/1760x1084!/format/webp/quality/90/?url=https%3A%2F%2Fmedia.npr.org%2Fassets%2Fimg%2F2022%2F11%2F22%2Fimage-4-1-_wide-bf198b204fe954565de816cfd85c7e6fdcd54a1e.jpeg)

There is also a constant reminder of fleeting time. Our daily life is too fast-paced and we are rushing to optimize every aspect of it. Long form videos and news have transitioned to shorts and reels, books turned into audiobooks (don't get me wrong, I love audiobooks) or book summaries, games investing in microtransactions, filling up the world with collectibles and mindless activity that you'd "never get bored of", and finally programming replaced with autocomplete and agentic coding. With all of that we're still running out of time. Our attention span is fragmented into tiny pieces that keep getting broken down further and further. Our desires also grow exponentially with this abudance of convenience. We want to build more projects, make more websites, finish more games, read more books, finish more tasks and JIRA tickets.

When I started Elden Ring, I went with the intention to "beat" the game. Finish all the regions. Defeat all the bosses. But the game is pretty quick to tell you to "put your foolish ambitions to rest". Even after defeating a minor boss, the brief moment of achievement has made me boastful and led to repeated deaths in just the next area (when you first go from Limgrave to Caelid, you'll know how it feels to go from La-La land to literal Hell).

![](https://preview.redd.it/hllhzc9w45n81.jpg?width=640&crop=smart&auto=webp&s=41cb434503406fc93e4abc7b3ec3339d08902d10)

But I think that's... kinda cool? It forces me to stop, take it slow, level up, do side quests. In other words, eat the frog. We humans are not the latest NVIDIA graphics cards. Being slow and methodical IS what defines us. It is what helps us grow. When it comes to writing software, it is true that what you want to build is most probably already built and is better than you will ever make it to be. It is also true that Claude or Codex can probably do it 100x better than you, or any senior engineer. But should that stop you from trying? Absolutely not. Write that shitty code, get your ass handed to you by the compiler, lose your sleep over messy and confusing docs, let an unsolved problem keep you awake at night. And most importantly, don't rush it. It's not a marathon. Doesn't matter if you have 1 finished project or 100, you're prrobably not gonna be satisfied if it is vibe coded. It is hard to feel the impact of something that you haven't built yourself, why would it?
Does this mean completely get rid of all the coding agents and write code by hand? Again, nope. The common argument is to use them for repetitive tasks, boilerplates, tests, UI. But I think there is a nuance to that. You need to feel the repetitiveness of a task before it becomes repetitive. You need to understand testing modules and fixtures before they become a chore. So if you can't think of anything that annoys you, I guess you need to continue gitting gud and farming lower class enemies on repeat.

It is also nice to ask AI questions. Just don't offload the human labour to it. As they say, patience is a virtue. It can be tempting to start parallely building all the projects that you ever thought of, now that we have an AI assistant. I know this because I myself have fallen victim to this mindset, with 5+ projects that I never finished, even with AI. You can't possibly divide your attention budget to exact equal number of fractions and allocate them to each project as you go. Much like how you cannot read two books at the same time (some people might be able to, I can't), or paint two paintings. We often forget that writing software is a creative endeavour on its own. Some of the greatest software (Linux, Git, RollerCoaster Tycoon) were written by one person. It doesn't mean they're flawless, far from it. But they wouldn't be possible if the authors split their brain throwing a thousand different ideas on the board and saw what sticks.

If you want to build something that scratches a particular itch in you, or just for learning something, that is a creative process where you pour your heart into. Have the conviction and faith in that vision, and give your brain the respect and the agency it deserves.


## Acceptance

![](https://preview.redd.it/first-weekend-down-how-many-runes-have-you-lost-so-far-v0-5yg7onosrlk81.png?auto=webp&s=1ca53718cc9443fbaa18012f617e8a1b65d406f5)

It is embarrassing how many times I have lost close to 50k runes just because I was being cocky. For non-gamers who are reading this, in Elden Ring (and most other soulslike games) you acquire runes by killing enemies and bosses. Its a currency to level up, upgrade weapons, armors, buy shit, etc. When you die you respawn at the last checkpoint but you lose all the runes you had before dying. You can go and collect them on your 2nd attempt as they can be collected from the same point on the map where you last died. **However**, if you die before acquiring them the 2nd time, they are lost forever.

For the longest time, I was focusing too much on preserving my sweet sweet runes, being very careful, tip-toeing around tough enemies, farming runes in beginner areas and just being cautious and comfortable. And it felt like uninstalling the game when I lost all the hard-earned runes on a ledge trying to platform into tricky areas, or getting falcon-punched by some nasty lad I thought I could take on. Honestly the game has a very profound way of humbling you in the most unexpected times, especially when you are just starting to feel quite powerful.

But there is no shortage of runes in the game. If you keep yourself stuck in the starting areas, the loots will not keep improving, the runes won't be much. Sure, you can keep grinding day and night, absolutely slaughter everyone in Limgrave, time and time again. But you can also get higher runes from more difficult areas like Caelid or Liurnia, where the risk is also higher.

Similarly, I think that written code also acts as the currency on our quest for learning. When I started out programming, I was focusing too much on writing the best, the most perfect code. I was feeling a false sense of accomplishment and comfort by writing the same familiar code over and over again. I was overly protective about my code and adverse to making any change.
The point I'm trying to make here, is that the growth comes from accepting the fact that the code itself is never important, at least not as important as the things you're learning.

So the question is, do you stay comfortable in mediocrity, or do you venture further deep?
